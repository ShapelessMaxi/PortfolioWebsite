<h2>Getting Started with Archival Research, Sage Publications</h2>
<p>https://researchmethodscommunity.sagepub.com/blog/getting-started-with-archival-research</p>
<p>This very short guide gave me insights on the outputs of archival research, and on basic methods to prepare my visit and stay organized during it. There are a few reasons for performing archival research that keep getting mentioned, here are a few: Research projects can benefits from a larger historical context, some archives may have more recent material which can help gaining an overall better understanding of a problem, we can derive new meaning and knowledge from old data, compare the problem overtime or between geographic areas, verify/challenge existing knowledge. This sparked an idea for the narrative of my game: Find historical events and compare them with current ones. You receive messages that guides you through the current event, i.e. "come to the park we are protesting against the undoing(? cant think of the actual word) of same-sex marriage", then upon arrival to the destination, a reconstruction of a past protest on a similar theme happens. Further in the text, the 'How can I get started?' section was really useful, and motivated me to plan my visit. For example, it made me want to email the archivist about relevant material, and scan their website for more information on the rules of conduct.</p>
<h2>A Guide to Archival Research, McMaster University Library</h2>
<p>This guide is a bit more in-depth, which I appreciate. It gives proper examples of how an archive might be structured, of a 'Finding Aid' booklet, and particular case study of the analysis of primary source documents. The part describing different types of primary sources was particularly helpful, as they also give you tips on what to keep in mind when using each type (i.e. photographs, remember that photographs have particular point of view and don't always tell the whole story.). I will for sure revisit The Archival Research Process Part 1: Before you visit the archives, as it will help me think through my research question before contacting the archivist for more help.</p>

<h1>Literature Review - 16/02</h1>
<h2>(Per)Forming Archival Research Methodologies (2012), Lynée Lewis Gaillet</h2>
<p>In this article, the author highlights different problems with archival research methodologies, archivists-researchers, and how archives are organized. Problems that were already evident in 1999, as Gaillet cites John Brereton's words:"[W]e still aren't sure what should be in our archive, or how access be broadened, or which tools we should bring to our task of exploring the past (p.36)". This point to a lingering issue, a lack of definition and consensus on the question of What is archival research? (Funnily enough, this was my exact question prior to starting this project). To wrap-up this intro, Gaillet points to Working in the Archives, a collection of essays that also try to answer the same questions (I will possibly read a few of them next). It seems like one way to answer this lack of definition is to accept the fact that there isn't one. Gaillet cites examples of innovative archival research that no one but the researcher thought could exist. She recounts the words of James O'Toole: "Any archivist who has supervised a collection knows that an ingenious researcher can find uses for records that no creator, collector, or curator ever imagined (p.39)".</p>
<p>Archives are then locales full of knowledge yet to be "discovered", or in other words, the materials in them can be reread in different ways. But having such an open framework certainly complicates other things. The subsequent problems are described further: Can a researcher perform archival research on a community they are not apart of? Which research methods are valid, useful, and rightful? How can we fact-check archival findings? and more.</p>
<p>Recounting the story of a community. "Undeniably, scholars bring a set of preconceived notions, experiences, and perspectives their topic choices (p.42)". While choosing a research project, Wendy Sharer, awarded archival-researcher, urges us to "blend personal experience and professional work (p.42)", because our emotional connections, passion, and respect for the community will most often result in a more nuanced, humane, and whole representation of what actually happened. The author then continues with another scholar David Gold. This time around, the awarded work concerned a community Gold wasn't apart of, highlighting supplementary research behaviors such as listening to members of the community, reading contemporary materials, triangulating data, and overall having a deep and sincere understanding and respect for the community can allow an outsider to speak on their behalf.</p>
<p>Valid interdisciplinary methodologies. In brief, most methods from most field are valid, but the researcher is expected to have done the necessary work to fully understand the borrowed methods or findings. This work can simply be to read about the methods and their use in the appropriate discipline, or it can be a learn by osmosis type of situation where working with researchers from different fields using these niche methods allow you to understand how to appropriate them for yourself. "Misunderstanding methodology or 'superficial borrowings' can easily lead to schisms [...] (p.45)".</p>
<p>Making sure we tell the right story. The author urges us to triangulate the data, a term used by archivists and researchers meaning that to be sure of a claim, there needs to be more than three primary sources pointing to that conclusion. The amount of sources is a suggestion, but the more the better. On the subject of triangulation, the author highlight the terms' connection with storytelling by citing Adams: "Archival research is all about storytelling because through all the documents you study, you are figuring out the nature of a life [...] (p.51)". From that, we can understand archival research as a story built by the researcher, and a story isn't always true. That's why some scholars bring in the actual piece of evidence for the reader of audience to interpret themselves.</p>
